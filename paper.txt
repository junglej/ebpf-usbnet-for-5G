Abstract
The cellular uplink is increasingly important for modern applications but poses significant challenges to congestion control algorithms (CCAs), due to the highly dynamic conditions and complex
operations. Existing CCA paradigms, mainly for downloading data,
are unable to react promptly to uplink fluctuations because they
rely on ACKs to indicate congestion, introducing inevitable control
loop latency. Furthermore, the cost associated with modifications
to both the sender and receiver cannot be overlooked.
We observe a fundamental opportunity in uplink scenarios: the
sender’s proximity to the wireless-side allows for fast and accurate
control without relying on ACK-based feedback, and requires no
modifications to either the receiver-side or the hardware layer. In
this paper, we propose Aether, mainly consisting of: (1) a monitor
deployed on the driver-layer that captures uplink dynamics, by
leveraging our key finding that driver buffer dynamics serve as
an indicator of uplink congestion; (2) a bottleneck detector that
activates Aether when the uplink becomes the bottleneck; (3) a rate
controller that steers transmission toward uplink capacity. We implement Aether on commercial devices and develop a trace-driven
emulator for testing Aether. Extensive evaluations demonstrate that
Aether outperforms state-of-the-art schemes, improving average
throughput by 9.28% to 234.11% while consistently reducing latency
by 5.34% to 56.94%.

Introduction

The cellular uplink is increasingly important in the current Internet
data transmission [30]. Emerging applications (e.g., crowdsourced
livestreaming [47], video conference [36], cloud gaming [15], mobile AI inference [59]) necessitate continuous data upload from the
client-side to the Cloud. When uploading data via cellular networks,
it is essential to ensure the efficient utilization of uplink capacity,
which often emerges as the bottleneck. Thus, mobile-side congestion control algorithms (CCAs) that govern the data upload rate
become a pivotal determinant of uploading performance.
However, cellular infrastructure is closed and employs complex
data scheduling [27, 29, 34, 44, 55]. Particularly in uplink scenarios,
this opacity and complexity pose significant challenges to CCA design, necessitating rapid responses to network dynamics and precise
rate control to safeguard transmission performance. Uplink transmission states, such as the modem status of user equipment (UE)
[54], handover timing [43], and physical-layer resource scheduling
[50], serve as critical indicators of wireless channel fluctuations.
Acquiring and leveraging this information is key to realizing efficient cellular CCAs. The rationale is straightforward: the CCA’s
sending rate must closely align with the data scheduling behaviors
of the cellular subsystems. This alignment ensures the full utilization of allocated wireless resources while preventing excessive data
queuing that would otherwise inflate self-induced delay.
Fine-grained cellular information is important to ensure transmission performance, but the timely acquisition of such radiooriginated information is non-trivial: ➊ Current ACK-based feedback designs are inefficient for uplink-bottlenecked transmission.

should be sender-centric, without requiring external coordination (e.g.,
receiver-side or infrastructure changes) or modem firmware modifications.
We bridge the above mismatches by developing Aether, a responsive and lightweight CCA for cellular uplink. While the key
challenge lies in capturing cellular network dynamics quickly and
accurately without modifying the cellular subsystem or the receiver
side, Aether addresses this through: ➊ Direct sender-side probing.
Leveraging the sender’s proximity to the air interface in uplink
scenarios, Aether directly probes cellular transmission states at
the UE, eliminating the unnecessary observation delay; ➋ Capturing cellular dynamics without modem firmware modifications.
Our key findings (§3) reveal that the driver-level sending queue
dynamics closely mirror the underlying modem data scheduling
behaviors. Thus, Aether deploys the monitor on the driver layer to
accurately capture the cellular dynamics (§4.3), avoiding modem
hardware/firmware modifications; ➌ Bottleneck detector and rate
adaptation. Aether applies a bottleneck detector (§4.4) that adaptively determines whether to invoke its rate controller based on the
current bottleneck location. Furthermore, using the uplink capacity
estimated by the packet driver-level dwell time (§4.5), Aether’s rate
controller quickly and precisely regulates the sending rate to adapt
to network fluctuations while maintaining consistently low latency.
We implement Aether on Android smartphones and Linux laptops, with both integrated and PCIe-based modem chipsets (e.g.,
Qualcomm SDX55, Qualcomm SDX75, MediaTek Dimensity 1080).
We develop a reproducible trace-driven emulator based on Mahimahi
[45], extending it with a module that emulates driver queue dynamics. We collected real-world traces to capture how driver queues fluctuate with network dynamics and used them to drive the emulation.
Both real-world and trace-driven results demonstrate that Aether
achieves the highest throughput and the lowest round-trip time
(RTT). Specifically, Aether outperformed representative CCAs such
as CUBIC [21], Copa+ [26], Cesar [50], and Proteus [42], achieving
throughput improvements up to 51.23%, 119.97%, 98.64%, 53.25%,
along with RTT reductions of 28.14%, 20.6%, 20.31%, 28.53%, respectively (§6.2). Our key contributions are:

Background

Transmission over cellular uplink. Default network operator
configurations are downlink-favored [50], and the uplink capacity
of LTE/5G is much lower than that of the downlink. We collected
real-world cellular traffic traces from 61 base stations (BSs) owned
by ChinaNet across a district of the Hangzhou urban area. The
measurement spanned two weeks, with daily observations conducted between 10:00 and 19:00. The bands of the base stations
include 800 MHz, 1.8 GHz, and 2.1 GHz. The maximum number of
concurrent uplink connections per BS ranges from 45 to 1,405. We
selected long-duration flows (>60s) corresponding to file transfers
and video streams, as such flows are more likely to saturate the
available uplink/downlink bandwidth. Moreover, the traces also
include our maintained continuous iperf-based upload/download
sessions to a local Cloud center (aiming to probe the maximum
bandwidth). Figure 2a shows the average uplink and downlink data
rate of the collected sessions. We can observe that the average
uploading bandwidth of users is 16.7%-18.1% of the downloading
bandwidth. Furthermore, the uploading bandwidth between different base stations is also highly varied, i.e., the bandwidth difference
can be up to 28.31 Mbps for the neighboring stations. The uplink
latency increases more rapidly than the downlink latency. Figure 2b
shows that the delay when using the uplink increases more rapidly
than that of the downlink. When the velocity reaches 80 km/h, the
maximum uplink latency is over twice that of the downlink.
Limitations of ACK-based feedback. Current CCAs targeting
cellular networks focus on data downloads, manifested by the assumption that the UE acts as the receiver. These methods rely on
feedback loops where cellular subsystem metrics are piggybacked
onto ACKs to drive the sender’s rate controller. For instance, C2TCP
[8] and Cesar [50] measure the cellular change via received ACKs,
PBE-CC [54] embeds physical-layer bandwidth information into
ACKs, while M2HO [38] utilizes ACKs to convey corrected window
size. However, the fast variation in the cellular network renders
these ACK-based feedback loops unable to keep pace with the actual
network evolution. Cellular network states evolve at sub-RTT granularity. For example, physical resource allocation in BSs operates
at the millisecond level [14] to track the rapid radio fluctuations.
Consequently, the discrepancy between the sender’s perception and
the ground truth accumulates over time, triggering self-induced
queue buildup and leading to congestion.

However, schemes relying on explicit feedback [20, 54] often necessitate cross-entity collaboration, requiring the deployment of
monitoring agents on UEs/BSs and modified CCAs on servers. Such
designs mandate dual-ended modifications and infrastructure-level
support, which face significant hurdles in production environments.
In the real world, the network ecosystem is highly siloed: service
providers update application logic, smartphone and chipset vendors
control the UE’s firmware/OS, and mobile operators operate the
infrastructure. Since the sender and receiver are managed by distinct administrative domains, bridging these diverse stakeholders to
implement a unified congestion control framework is operationally
infeasible. Consequently, the reliance on multi-party participation
renders these solutions largely theoretical, limiting their viability
in large-scale deployment.
In fact, we argue that the reliance on ACK-based feedback
is unnecessary in uplink scenarios. Since the sender in uplink
scenarios is the UE, CCAs are able to access cellular states locally
and instantaneously, eliminating the feedback latency inherent in
network traversal. Leveraging this locality, a superior strategy is to
monitor network fluctuations at the device side and directly expose
them to the CCAs within the OS kernel. In the next section, we
demonstrate how the modem driver buffer status can serve as an
accurate, real-time signal for adjusting the sending rate.

Key Observations

Observation 1: Significant challenges in acquiring and analyzing cellular air interface operations. Current approaches [38, 54]
mainly use signaling messages collected at the MAC/physical layer
to measure the dynamics. However, acquiring such fine-grained
signaling messages is non-trivial. Capturing cellular signaling messages relies on specialized diagnostic tools for interception and
parsing. Such a diagnostic tool [34] can incur an extra CPU overhead, reaching 12%–23%. Crucially, the processing overhead is proportional to the message volume. When signaling messages surge
during complex scenarios such as handovers (peaking at 20–30
messages per second), the computational burden is amplified. Most

existing methods for real-time signaling collection necessitate intrusive modifications at the modem hardware/firmware level. In
practice, the vast majority of smartphone vendors utilize proprietary baseband chipsets supplied by third-party manufacturers,
making these methods difficult for deployment on commercial UEs.
Observation 2: Modem driver buffer status timely and accurately characterize cellular uplink status. To bypass the
limitations of invasive modem firmware modifications and avoid
the overhead of control signaling analysis, we scrutinize the data
scheduling behavior within the modem subsystem. We collect realworld traces by leveraging vendor-supplied diagnostic tools and
deploying hooks within the kernel TCP stack. These traces specify
the packet states and processing dynamics at both the transport
layer and the modem chipset. We select several traces and showcase the results in Figure 2c. With the degradation of the physicallayer transmission rate, the packet dwell time, which is termed as
the time interval from packet entry into the modem buffer until
the occupied buffer is freed, rises rapidly. Cellular uplink applies
grant-based access (driven by Buffer Status Reports to the BS) and
acknowledgment mechanisms to guarantee transmission reliability
[37, 57]. Consequently, upon entering the cellular subsystem, packets remain buffered until acknowledged. Hence, network quality
fluctuations are directly mirrored in modem transmission queue dynamics. The figure further reveals a distinct lag in TCP sending rate
adjustments compared to the cellular physical-layer transmission
rate. This latency arises because CCAs, such as BBR and CUBIC,
are ACK-driven. The feedback from the receiver inherently delays
the response to these network changes.
To demonstrate the efficiency of monitoring modem transmission queuing, we build a proof-of-concept test: We implement userspace BBR/CUBIC, and feed the modem dwell time to these CCAs.
We modified the CCA’s pacing rate update logic to use a weighted
average (with a coefficient of 0.5) of two components: the standard pacing rate derived from ACKs, and the rate estimated by
modem dwell time. As validated by real-world testing, Figure 2d
demonstrates measurable improvements compared with the vanilla
version of BBR and CUBIC. However, accessing the queuing status

Observation 3: Correlation between driver queue and modem
transmission buffer. We further investigate the hardware architectures and driver code of mainstream chips, such as those from
Qualcomm, with the details summarized in Table 1 of Appendix
A. Data interaction between the modem driver and the chipsets
is facilitated by a DMA-based ring buffer. Taking the Qualcomm
SDX55/65 chipset family as an example, as shown in Figure 3, packets delivered by the kernel stack are mapped to this shared ring
buffer (TX ring) via the ndo_start_xmit function. Once the data
is successfully enqueued into the modem’s internal transmission
queue, the driver releases the corresponding TX ring space using
the function mhi_net_ul_callback.
Since the data transfer from the ring buffer to the modem’s
DRAM occurs via the PCIe/NoC interface, the transfer latency is
negligible (microsecond level). Consequently, queue buildup at the
driver level is primarily dictated by backpressure originating from
the modem’s on-chip transmission queue. Crucially, our trace analysis validates that when the uplink becomes the bottleneck, the
packet residence time within the driver-layer buffer tightly couples
with that inside the modem’s internal transmission queue. Figure 4a
indicates that when the wireless link becomes the transmission bottleneck, the queue buildup in the driver layer exhibits a high degree
of consistency with the state of the modem’s internal transmission buffer. Figure 4b further confirms a strong positive correlation
between the two dwell times across all traces. More results are
provided in Figure 16 in Appendix A. Under uplink saturation, the
driver and modem transmission buffer effectively function as a
single logical queue, resulting in consistent dwell time dynamics
across both layers. A detailed formal proof is provided in Appendix A. Thus, packet driver-level dwell time can accurately infer
the real-time cellular congestion level without requiring complex
hardware/firmware changes.

ACK-based feedback, monitoring the driver-layer buffering provides explicit signals that can immediately notify the sender at
the beginning of wireless bottleneck queuing. Furthermore, tracking driver-layer operations does not require modifications to UE

Cellular uplink-aware rate control. Leveraging this driver-layer
tracking, Aether uses the packet driver-level dwell time to measure
the uplink condition changes and control the sending rate. The
driver-level dwell time serves as an accurate and responsive proxy
for the uplink congestion. It reflects the queue buildup at the uplink
bottleneck, regardless of the underlying network environments
or hardware configurations. Additionally, driver-level dwell time
facilitates bottleneck localization without requiring network-side
support. For example, by comparing the driver-level dwell time with
RTT, the sender can distinguish whether congestion is stemming
from the radio side or the Internet.
Non-goals and scope of Aether. Aether is designed specifically to
boost cellular uplink transmission performance, rather than being
a general-purpose CCA. This focus stems from the pronounced
asymmetry between uplink and downlink characteristics. In practice, cellular radio resources are heavily skewed towards the downlink, leaving the uplink with “just enough” resources [18]. This
makes the uplink far more sensitive to wireless dynamics as we
discussed in §3, requiring additional, dedicated means to ensure
rapid responsiveness. Downlink and uplink CCAs also diverge in
design purpose. Server-side senders in downlink, with abundant
resources, can afford computationally intensive algorithms (e.g.,
learning-based [17, 24, 42]) and are optimized for specific applications. Constrained by limited hardware resources and diverse
application requirements, uplink CCAs must remain lightweight
yet resilient. Notably, our uplink-centric design does not preclude
downlink utility. We further elaborate on adapting our mechanism
to downlink case in Appendix G.

4.3

Transmission Status Monitor

Transmission status monitor addresses two key problems: ➊ how
to monitor the driver-layer operations; ➋ which signals should be
monitored to indicate uplink dynamics.
Driver-layer operations monitoring. To solve the first problem,
we instrument the driver-level transmission queue (TX ring buffer)
by placing hooks on its key manipulation routines (Enqueue and
Dequeue function calls). To better illustrate the design, we use the
open-source Linux kernel, which is also widely used by UEs (as the
upstream OS kernel of Android), as an example. According to the
device tree, the host interface of the modem mainly uses on-chip
interconnects (e.g., Snapdragon 8 Elite, MediaTek Dimensity 1080)
and external buses like PCIe (e.g., Snapdragon 865 with SDX55)
or USB (Quectel chipsets). Distinct host interconnection interfaces
correspond to specific driver modules within the Linux kernel:
mhi_net for PCIe-based, rmnet_ipa for SoC-based, qmi_wwan for
USB. UEs such as smartphones and laptops primarily adopt SoCintegrated or PCIe-based interconnections, driven by the need for
high-speed data processing (see Appendix B).
Although these drivers differ in function organization and variable naming, their design logic for operating the TX ring is consistent. The analysis of driver source code shows that the data
backlog dynamics within the TX ring are manipulated by packet

transmit function calls (enqueue) and modem consumption signals
(dequeue). In Appendix B, we showcase the related function calls
in different drivers. As described in §3, in cellular networks, the
driver’s dequeue routine is tightly coupled with the modem’s physical operation. Therefore, the invocation timing of this function
is correlated with the uplink dynamics. Other mobile platforms,
such as iOS and HarmonyOS, share consistent underlying cellular
hardware architectures and bus protocols. Therefore, analogous
transmission behaviors are observable across different platforms.
Indicators of cellular uplink dynamics. For the second problem,
the metrics that vary with the air interface are the queue length
and the driver-level dwell time (the time from when an individual
packet enters the queue until it is removed). The maximum length
of the skb_buffer differs by modem type and driver version (e.g., 4
for USB, 128 for PCIe). Therefore, if queue length were used as the
sole CCAs parameter (i.e., using a queue-length threshold to decide
whether to signal uplink congestion), each modem would require a
customized algorithm to accommodate its specific maximum driver
queue length. Instead, we use driver-level dwell time. This indicator
can be regarded as the packet’s sub-rtt on the air interface; using
this sub-rtt makes it easier to estimate the current air-interface
capacity and determine whether the air interface is the bottleneck.
In addition, the gradient of this residence time can be used as a key
indicator to report whether the network status of the air interface
is changing.

4.4

Bottleneck Detector

Cellular network conditions fluctuate rapidly, which inevitably
causes the bottleneck to shift from the wireless side to the nonwireless side during transmission. Aether detects the bottleneck
location based on the observed driver-level dwell time and packetlevel end-to-end latency.
Bottleneck shifting. We define two types of bottleneck: uplink
bottleneck, where the cellular uplink is the bottleneck of the connection, Internet bottleneck for otherwise. When the TCP sending rate
exceeds the modem’s output rate, the uplink becomes the transmission bottleneck. When the bottleneck shifts to uplink, the driver
queue builds up quickly and driver-level dwell time increases continuously. Our analysis shows that the driver queuing up arises
primarily during startup or abrupt changes in network conditions,
for example: (1) When a UE begins to upload data, UE sends a
Buffer Status Report (BSR) and awaits a scheduling grant from
the BSs prior to actual data transmission; (2) When the allocable
Physical Resource Blocks (PRBs) decrease because the number of
users served by the BS has increased; (3) When a packet needs to
retransmit at the cellular MAC layer due to receiving of negative
acknowledgment. Conversely, when the bottleneck shifts to Internet bottleneck, the driver’s transmission buffer drains rapidly and
driver-level dwell time falls to very small values.
Dealing with different bottlenecks. When uplink bottleneck is
detected, Aether directly activates its uplink-dedicated rate controller (§4.5). When in Internet bottleneck, the uplink capacity may
exceed the actual bottleneck bandwidth. Determining the sending
rate based on uplink capacity may increase self-induced latency. To
avoid this, the bottleneck detector immediately switches control to
the default CCA.

the lack of visibility into MAC-layer scheduling patterns [11]. We

correlated. While this metric serves as a proxy rather than a precise

for detecting link bottlenecks. Let driver-level dwell time difference
in consecutive packets be denoted by Δ𝐷𝑘 : 𝐷𝑘 − 𝐷𝑘 −1 . Thus, when
we observe Δ𝐷𝑘 increase rapidly and rtt𝑛𝑜𝑛−𝑘 remains static, or

Internet bottleneck. Our evaluation (§6.4) confirms the effectiveness
of using driver-level dwell time to estimate bottleneck conditions.
When UEs initialize a data uploading session, the bottleneck detector enters the uplink bottleneck by default. This design has two
advantages: ➊ for most of the time, cellular uplink is the bottleneck
of the established connections, as we discussed in §2; ➋ Entering
uplink bottleneck triggers Aether control logic. Aether’s rate controller (§4.5) can quickly converge to the actual uplink bandwidth,
avoiding the startup data surges that would otherwise build a long
transmission queue.

4.6

Design rationales

In the following, we discuss several key design choices of Aether.
Window updating. Regarding the design of the multiplicative
factor, instead of directly adopting the ratio 𝐶𝑢 /𝑟 , we integrate a
logarithmic form. This design choice is motivated by the following
considerations: 𝐶𝑢 /𝑟 spikes sharply during the startup phase, leading to severe cwnd oscillations, which defeats the purpose of smooth
pacing when designing CCAs. The logarithmic design dampens
these volatile spikes, fostering a more moderate transition while
preserving robust convergence when approaching 𝐶𝑢 .
Bottleneck detection methods. To maintain the non-intrusiveness
into the cellular subsystem, Aether cannot directly deploy physicallayer-based methods, such as BurstTracker [11], to locate the bottleneck. An alternative is the dual-control-loop design, where the
default congestion control and Aether independently compute window sizes and select the minimum as the definitive cwnd. This
approach can miss critical cwnd-growth opportunities. We instead
directly measure the driver-layer queue dwell time against the total
rtt. When the cellular link becomes the bottleneck, the variance of
the driver-layer queuing delay will dominate the rtt fluctuations,
providing a robust and distinct signature for localized bottleneck
identification.
Interaction with Default CCAs. Upon detecting the Internet bottleneck, control is handed back to the native CCAs, requiring a
well-defined interaction between Aether and other CCAs. Aether
enables native CCAs to smoothly recover from its operating state.
Since Aether tracks wireless capacity (which is currently nonbottlenecked), thus, the actual end-to-end throughput may be lower
than Aether’s estimation, the native CCAs probe the new available

bandwidth. Taking CUBIC and BBR as examples, upon identifying
the Internet as the bottleneck, CUBIC directly inputs the cwnd computed by Aether as the last maximum window size into its CUBIC
function for window updating. BBR enters the ProbeBW phase to
detect new bandwidth values. Other CCAs can similarly recover
their respective cwnd from Aether.

Implementation

Aether confines all modifications strictly to the kernel space, requiring no modifications to the UEs’ hardware/firmware, BSs, receiver,
or applications. The implementation overview is shown in Figure 6.
We present the deployment of Aether on Linux kernel-based operating systems (including Android, the dominant mobile platform).
The implementation consists of three major components:
Monitor. The core driver interacting with the modem is rmnet_
ipa.c, which adheres to the Linux Net_device_ops structure and
defines device behaviors. The enqueue operation of driver TX buffer
is handled by ipa3_wwan_xmit (corresponding to the Linux interface ndo_start_xmit). The dequeue operation is apps_ipa_tx_
complete_notify. This function is called when the hardware releases the TX buffer, signifying the completion of hardware processing. To measure the driver-level dwell time, Aether hooks these
function calls and logs invocation timestamps alongside packet
metadata. We import the driver-level dwell time and buffer length
into the kernel global symbol table via EXPORT_SYMBOL, allowing
direct access by the kernel modules bottleneck detector and rate
controller of Aether.
Bottleneck detector and rate controller. The Bottleneck Detector is deployed in the form of a kernel module. Bottleneck detector reads driver-level dwell time and buffer length from EXPORT_SYMBOL to determine whether the link is currently a bottleneck according to the logic in §4.4. The rate controller modifies
the bottleneck signal bit accordingly. For rate controller’s implementation, we directly add Aether’s code (Algorithm 1) to the existing kernel CCAs. Taking CUBIC as an example, we embed the
cwnd updates of Aether into the rate control parts in cubic.c, i.e.,
bictcp_update. We use the bottleneck signal bit as the condition for
entering this logic: when the bottleneck signal bit is 1, the system
enters Aether’s rate controlling logic instead of adopting CUBIC’s
original window adjustment method.
We currently implement Aether mainly on Android phones with
Qualcomm chipsets (integrated modem), Linux laptops (PCIe-based
modem), and Android phones with MediaTek chipsets. The kernel

TCP friendliness. Figure 13 illustrates the average throughput
ratios (evaluated CCA vs. legacy CCA) across varying numbers of
competing flows in real-world cellular networks. As shown in Figure 13a, Aether demonstrates near-ideal strict fairness with CUBIC,
consistently maintaining a throughput ratio around 1 regardless of
the flow count, avoiding the starvation or severe degradation issues
prevalent in other baselines. Conversely, when competing with BBR
(Figure 13b), Aether achieves a throughput ratio strictly greater
than 1. Importantly, this higher ratio does not imply that Aether is
unfairly aggressive. As discussed previously, BBR fundamentally
struggles with accurate bandwidth estimation in highly variable
cellular environments, leaving the wireless capacity underutilized.
Rather than starving competing BBR flows, Aether leverages its
uplink capacity probing to efficiently reclaim and utilize this idle
headroom, maximizing its own performance while ensuring benign
coexistence.
Robustness to sudden network changes. We design an experiment based on moving across different conditions. We first operate
UEs for 60 seconds in a good-signal location (RSRP of -90 dBm), then
quickly move to a poor-signal location (RSRP of -110 dBm) with

constant speed, and operate for 60 seconds. Then UEs travel back
along the same trajectory and at the same speed to the good-signal
location. We measure two key metrics over 100 repeated runs: ➊
Convergence time, time to converge to a stable rate (within 10%
variance) after entering poor signal conditions; ➋ Recovery time,
time to converge to a stable rate with 90% of its original steadystate level when back to good-signal location. Aether- indicates
the Aether without the bottleneck detector and always controls the
rate by Aether rate controller.
As shown in Figure 14a, Aether responds rapidly to sudden network changes, achieving a convergence time of just 1.44s and a
recovery time of only 1.77s. By disabling the bottleneck detector
in Aether-, the convergence and recovery times slow significantly
to 3.52s and 2.23s, respectively. Notably, even without the Bottleneck Detector, Aether remains competitive with conventional
algorithms.
Overhead. We also validate the computational overhead to UEs
caused by CCAs. Each CCA runs continuously for 300 seconds
under identical conditions, with detailed measurements of average
CPU utilization recorded and presented in Figure 14b. CCAs are implemented in the Linux kernel space, such as CUBIC, BBR, C2TCP,
Cesar, Aether, and demonstrates significantly lower CPU utilization. These algorithms benefit from their ability to achieve rapid
responses by precisely processing, thereby avoiding unnecessary
computational overhead. In contrast, Vivace, which also operates
in kernel space, incurs substantially higher computational costs

due to its online learning-based control strategy that involves complex parameter adjustments and consequently prolongs the control
loop cycle. Despite also being learning-based, Astraea demonstrates
lower computational overhead, which is likely owing to its optimized C++ implementation.
Impact of parameters in our algorithm. Aether relies on 𝐾 to
send probing packets in the empty queue case, and 𝛼, 𝛾 for tuning

Related Work

CCAs driven by end-to-end signals. CCA designs are constantly
evolving to adapt to increasingly complex and diverse network
conditions. Many of the CCAs [10, 12, 13, 22, 25, 26, 41, 53] use
end-to-end signals such as RTT, packet loss to control the data sending rate, attempting to achieve optimal transmission performance
across a wide variety of network conditions. Recently, an increasing number of learning-based CCAs [17, 24, 35, 40, 42, 51, 56, 61]
have emerged. By using sophisticated models, these approaches
leverage various learning methods, such as reinforcement learning
(Astraea [35], Aurora [24]) and online learning (PCC Vivace [17],
PCC Proteus [42]), aiming to generate control policies that are more
suitable for the target network environment.
CCAs for wireless networks. In wireless networks, high-precision
and fast-reacting transmission is often achieved by understanding
radio-side operations. In relatively open and programmable WiFi environments, CCA solutions [32, 43] read the queue occupancy from
the NIC/AP to optimize the sending rate. In contrast, cellular infrastructure is inherently a blackbox to CCAs. Studies [8, 31, 50, 53, 58]
use transport-layer feedback to infer how UEs and BS operate. Representative works, such as C2TCP [8], mitigate wireless dynamics
by estimating the minimum packet delay. Cesar [50] infers BS resource scheduling policy from TCP ACK inter-arrival times. The
rapid fluctuations and complex low-layer operations in cellular
networks make it difficult to accurately infer network conditions
based purely on ACK arriving patterns, since the causes of latency
are multifaceted. PEPesc [33] proposes an approach based on TCP
performance enhancing proxies (PEPs) for cellular backhaul, which
uses streaming coding to mask random packet losses and estimates
available bandwidth to control the data rate.
CCAs driven by radio-side control signaling. CCAs such as
ABC [20], PBE-CC [54], M2HO [38] access the cellular control signaling generated by the cellular subsystem. However, these works
primarily focus on downlink scenarios, necessitating the use of
ACKs to convey radio-side information back to the sender, which

Wang et al.

risks information obsolescence caused by network latency. Furthermore, the requirement for dual-sided modifications to convey ACK
feedback, combined with the hardware dependencies for accessing control signaling, raises deployment concerns. Their research
complements ours, as we pivot to the cellular uplink, a scenario
that has become increasingly important today. Our insight reveals
that in uplink transmission, CCAs should exploit the proximity
advantage to capture cellular changes locally and instantaneously.
This eliminates the dependency on ACK feedback and facilitates a
design without the need for intrusive hardware modifications.

8

Conclusion

Unlike transmission over cellular downlink, the inefficiency of existing CCAs in the uplink stems not from a lack of visibility into
cellular operations, but rather from the high latency and overhead involved in feeding these dynamics back to the CCAs. Simple but useful, Aether is proposed as an uplink-tailored CCA that
promptly captures uplink dynamics via modem driver-buffer status, motivated by the insight that driver-level dwell time serves
as an indicator of uplink congestion, and uses the rapid convergence to the optimal rate while sustaining consistently low latency,
within highly dynamic network conditions. The primary goal of
Aether is to achieve responsive, lightweight congestion control in
resource-constrained uplink environments, without requiring any
modifications beyond the sender’s OS kernel. Extensive evaluations
using both real-world experiments and trace-driven simulations
confirm Aether’s effectiveness in this regard. Aether provides an
advantageous option that addresses the growing demand for highperformance transmission over cellular uplinks.
Our future work branches into several directions. First, we plan to
conduct more comprehensive evaluations under extreme boundary
conditions, such as high-frequency bottleneck transitions, alongside
validating parameter robustness across diverse scenarios. Second,
efforts will be directed toward extending Aether’s deployment to a
broader range of network drivers. Third, we aim to refine the design
of cellular-dedicated trace-driven emulators, striving to align their
replay fidelity closer with real-world network counterparts. Finally,
we will explore fusing driver-buffer queue dynamics with upperlayer cellular telemetry to achieve a multi-dimensional awareness
of cellular network status. Due to space constraints, a more comprehensive discussion and detailed elaboration on these future
directions are provided in Appendix G.
Ethics: This work does not raise any ethical issues.
